# Provocation seeds

Each seed names a move. It only becomes a provocation once a specific finding from the research sits underneath it; a seed with nothing under it is a slogan, not a brief.

### Name the enemy
Not a competitor's product: the belief, habit, or default the whole category shares. "Every calendar app treats your time as a resource" names an enemy. "Beat Reclaim.ai" does not.

### Say what the category is embarrassed about
The thing every competitor keeps off their own landing page, sitting in plain sight in a review, a forum thread, or a support ticket.

### The format nobody in the category uses
A shape none of the five to eight competitors ships: the real customer email, the comments-off video, the rejection letter read out loud.

### Admit the flaw first
Open with the product's own weakest point before a reviewer gets to say it first, and dare the reader to still care.

### Write for the person who hates the category
Address the reader who already avoids or resents this category, not the reader who is shopping inside it.

### Take the complaint everyone has and make it the whole pitch
The verbatim complaint from section 2, repeated across multiple reviewers, becomes the headline instead of staying the fine print.

### Refuse the superlative
Every competitor claims "ultimate," "best," or "number one." State the smaller, truer claim instead, and let the refusal itself be the point.

### The platform risk nobody mentions
A rule, a rejection policy, or a grandfather clause the whole category operates under and no competitor names in its own marketing.

### The sober friend
The person the category's marketing never designs for: the one who isn't drinking, isn't playing, isn't buying, and gets no feature, no mode, no line of copy of their own.

### Make the disclaimer the product
The boilerplate legal or safety line every competitor tacks on with no feature behind it becomes the product's actual mechanic.

## Building the three unsafe briefs

At least two of the three unsafe briefs are built from a specific research finding: a named complaint with a URL, a thing every competitor avoids saying, a venue in-joke, or a gap between the category's promise and its reviews. Cite the finding in the brief.

The third brief may use a seed alone, with no finding under it, only when the research pass came back thin. Mark it as such in the debate minutes when this happens.

The decoy brief is fixed text, used unchanged every run:

> the safest, most competent direction a good agency would sell

## The author brief

Hand each unsafe candidate's author sub-agent this shape.

```
Input:
- The pack (manifesto sections 1 to 4).
- The provocation: one paragraph naming the seed move and the finding it is built on.
- The finding it cites: the complaint, gap, in-joke, or avoided statement, with its URL.

Output, at most 200 words, in the manifesto's Direction shape (section 5):
Name: <the candidate's name>
Enemy: <the belief or default this direction opposes>
Kind of unsafe: <derived from this product and this builder's taste>
Recurring move: <the one thing every piece for this candidate would do>
Three things competitors cannot say: <1> <2> <3>
What it is willing to lose: <which readers, which kind of comment>
Provocation and finding: <the seed move used, and the finding it was built on>
```
